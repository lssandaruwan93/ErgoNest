How to Set Up an Ergonomic Home Office

Published 1 January 2026 Updated 1 January 2026 9 min read

This article may contain affiliate links. If you buy through them, ErgoNest may earn a commission at no extra cost to you. Read the full disclosure.

Setting up a home office is mostly a sequence problem. Most people adjust whatever is easiest to reach first, which usually means the monitor. But monitor height only makes sense once your seated position is settled, and your seated position depends on the chair.

Quick answer

Work in this order: chair → desk height → monitor → keyboard and mouse → lighting → cables. Each step assumes the one before it is already correct.

This guide walks through that order. None of it requires special equipment, and most of it costs nothing.

1. Start with the chair

The chair sets the reference point for everything else. Get it wrong and every subsequent adjustment is a workaround.

Seat height

Sit with your feet flat on the floor. Your hips should be level with — or very slightly above — your knees. If your chair is too high, your feet will dangle and your thighs will take pressure. If it is too low, your knees rise and your lower back rounds.

Seat depth

There should be a small gap — roughly a fist's width — between the back of your knees and the front edge of the seat. If the seat presses into the back of your legs, it is too deep. If you feel like you are perched, it is too shallow.

Back support

The backrest should meet your lower back, not your shoulder blades. If it sits too high, it pushes your shoulders forward. If it sits too low, it does nothing.

Note

If your chair cannot be adjusted to meet these conditions, a footrest or a cushion can sometimes close the gap. Otherwise the chair itself is the limiting factor.

2. Match the desk to your elbows

With the chair settled, rest your arms at your sides and bend your elbows to roughly 90 degrees. Your desk surface should sit at about that height — level with your forearms when your shoulders are relaxed.

Common signs the desk is wrong:

  • Too high: your shoulders creep upward, or you raise your chair and your feet leave the floor.
  • Too low: you lean forward over the desk, rounding your upper back.

If the desk is not adjustable and is too high, raising the chair and adding a footrest is often the practical fix. If it is too low, desk risers or a desk converter are options.

3. Position the monitor

Screen position is one of the most commonly mis-set parts of a home office.

Height

The top of the screen should sit roughly at eye level, or slightly below. A common working rule is that your eyes should land on the top third of the screen when you are sitting comfortably upright — not craning up, not looking down.

Distance

A rough starting point is an arm's length from your eyes to the screen. If the screen feels like it requires leaning in, it is probably too far. If you find yourself pushing back, it may be too close.

Tilt

A slight backward tilt — just a few degrees — often reduces glare and keeps the screen perpendicular to your line of sight.

4. Keyboard and mouse

With the desk at the right height, your keyboard and mouse should sit within easy reach without stretching.

  • Keep the keyboard close enough that your elbows stay near your sides.
  • Keep the mouse on the same surface, at roughly the same height as the keyboard.
  • Keep your wrists roughly straight rather than angled up or down.

Common mistake

Resting your wrists on a hard desk edge while typing. A wrist rest can help position your hands, but it is intended to support pauses, not to be leaned on continuously while typing.

5. Lighting and glare

Light affects comfort over a long day, but it is often ignored until it becomes a problem.

  • Position lamps so light does not reflect directly off the screen into your eyes.
  • If there is a window behind you, it will reflect in the screen — consider moving the desk or using a blind.
  • If the window is in front of you, it may be brighter than the screen, which forces your eyes to keep readjusting.

6. Cable management

Cable clutter is not an ergonomic problem in itself, but it contributes to how usable a desk feels and how easy it is to adjust things later.

  • Route cables along the back edge of the desk, not across the working surface.
  • Bundle related cables together so they move as one when you adjust the desk.
  • Leave slack where cables need to flex — especially on a height-adjustable desk.
  • Fix the bundle to the underside of the desk rather than to the wall.

Full checklist

  • Feet flat on the floor, hips level with or slightly above knees
  • Small gap between the seat edge and the back of your knees
  • Backrest supporting the lower back, not the shoulder blades
  • Elbows at roughly 90 degrees, forearms level with the desk
  • Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level
  • Screen about an arm's length away
  • Keyboard and mouse within easy reach, wrists roughly straight
  • No direct reflections on the screen from lamps or windows
  • Cables routed away from the working surface
